# Chapter 7 Estimation

If we wish to estimate the mean *μ* of a population for which a census is impractical, say the average height of all 18-year-old men in the country, a reasonable strategy is to take a sample, compute its mean $\overset{-}{x}$, and estimate the unknown number *μ* by the known number $\overset{-}{x}.$ For example, if the average height of 100 randomly selected men aged 18 is 70.6 inches, then we would say that the average height of all 18-year-old men is (at least approximately) 70.6 inches.

Estimating a population parameter by a single number like this is called **point estimation**; in the case at hand the statistic $\overset{-}{x}$ is a **point estimate** of the parameter *μ*. The terminology arises because a single number corresponds to a single point on the number line.

A problem with a point estimate is that it gives no indication of how reliable the estimate is. In contrast, in this chapter we learn about **interval estimation**. In brief, in the case of estimating a population mean *μ* we use a formula to compute from the data a number *E*, called the margin of error[^stats-1] of the estimate, and form the interval $\left\lbrack {\overset{-}{x} - E,\overset{-}{x} + E} \right\rbrack.$ We do this in such a way that a certain proportion, say 95%, of all the intervals constructed from sample data by means of this formula contain the unknown parameter *μ*. Such an interval is called a **95%** confidence interval[^stats-2] **for** *μ*.

Continuing with the example of the average height of 18-year-old men, suppose that the sample of 100 men mentioned above for which $\overset{-}{x} = 70.6$ inches also had sample standard deviation *s* = 1.7 inches. It then turns out that *E* = 0.33 and we would state that we are 95% confident that the average height of all 18-year-old men is in the interval formed by $70.6 \pm 0.33$ inches, that is, the average is between 70.27 and 70.93 inches. If the sample statistics had come from a smaller sample, say a sample of 50 men, the lower reliability would show up in the 95% confidence interval being longer, hence less precise in its estimate. In this example the 95% confidence interval for the same sample statistics but with *n* = 50 is $70.6 \pm 0.47$ inches, or from 70.13 to 71.07 inches.

## 7.1 Large Sample Estimation of a Population Mean

````````````{note} Learning Objectives

1.  To become familiar with the concept of an interval estimate of the population mean.
2.  To understand how to apply formulas for a confidence interval for a population mean.

````````````

The Central Limit Theorem says that, for large samples (samples of size *n* ≥ 30), when viewed as a random variable the sample mean $\overset{-}{X}$ is normally distributed with mean $\mathit{\mu}_{\overset{-}{X}} = \mathit{\mu}$ and standard deviation $\left. \mathit{\sigma}_{\overset{-}{X}} = \mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}. \right.$ The Empirical Rule says that we must go about two standard deviations from the mean to capture 95% of the values of $\overset{-}{X}$ generated by sample after sample. A more precise distance based on the normality of $\overset{-}{X}$ is 1.960 standard deviations, which is $\left. E = 1.960\mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}. \right.$

The key idea in the construction of the 95% confidence interval is this, as illustrated in [Figure 7.1 \"When Winged Dots Capture the Population Mean\"](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s01-f01): because in sample after sample 95% of the values of $\overset{-}{X}$ lie in the interval $\left\lbrack {\mathit{\mu} - E,\mathit{\mu} + E} \right\rbrack$, if we adjoin to each side of the point estimate $\overset{-}{x}$ a "wing" of length *E*, 95% of the intervals formed by the winged dots contain *μ*. The 95% confidence interval is thus $\left. \overset{-}{x} \pm 1.960\mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}. \right.$ For a different level of confidence[^stats-3], say 90% or 99%, the number 1.960 will change, but the idea is the same.

It is standard practice to identify the level of confidence in terms of the area $\alpha$ in the two tails of the distribution of $\overset{-}{X}$ when the middle part specified by the level of confidence is taken out. This is shown in [Figure 7.3](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s01-f03), drawn for the general situation, and in [Figure 7.4](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s01-f04), drawn for 95% confidence. Remember from [Section 5.4.1 \"Tails of the Standard Normal Distribution\"](/chapters/ch-05-continuous-random-variables.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch05-s04-s01) in [Chapter 5 \"Continuous Random Variables\"](/chapters/ch-05-continuous-random-variables.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch05) that the *z*-value that cuts off a right tail of area *c* is denoted *z<sub>c</sub>*. Thus the number 1.960 in the example is $z_{.025}$, which is $z_{\alpha / 2}$ for $\alpha = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05.$

For 95% confidence the area in each tail is $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.025. \right.$

The level of confidence can be any number between 0 and 100%, but the most common values are probably 90% ($\alpha = 0.10$), 95% ($\alpha = 0.05$), and 99% ($\alpha = 0.01$).

Thus in general for a $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)$% confidence interval, $E = z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n} \right)$, so the formula for the confidence interval is $\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n} \right).$ While sometimes the population standard deviation *σ* is known, typically it is not. If not, for *n* ≥ 30 it is generally safe to approximate *σ* by the sample standard deviation *s*.

````````````{important} Large Sample $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)\%$ Confidence Interval for a Population Mean

If *σ* is known: $\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{\mathit{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$

If *σ* is unknown: $\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$

A sample is considered large when *n* ≥ 30.

````````````

As mentioned earlier, the number $\left. E = z_{\alpha / 2}\mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n} \right.$ or $\left. E = z_{\alpha / 2}s/\sqrt{n} \right.$ is called the *margin of error* of the estimate.

### Example 1

Find the number $z_{\alpha / 2}$ needed in construction of a confidence interval:

1.  when the level of confidence is 90%;
2.  when the level of confidence is 99%.

Solution:

1.  For confidence level 90%, $\alpha = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$, so $z_{\alpha / 2} = z_{0.05}.$ The procedure for finding this number was given in [Section 5.4.1 \"Tails of the Standard Normal Distribution\"](/chapters/ch-05-continuous-random-variables.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch05-s04-s01). Since the area under the standard normal curve to the right of $z_{.05}$ is 0.05, the area to the left of *z*<sub>0.05</sub> is 0.95. We search for the area 0.9500 in [Figure 12.2 \"Cumulative Normal Probability\"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f02). The closest entries in the table are 0.9495 and 0.9505, corresponding to *z*-values 1.64 and 1.65. Since 0.95 is exactly halfway between 0.9495 and 0.9505 we use the average 1.645 of the *z*-values for *z*<sub>0.05</sub>.
2.  For confidence level 99%, $\alpha = 1 - 0.99 = 0.01$, so $z_{\alpha / 2} = z_{0.005}.$ Since the area under the standard normal curve to the right of *z*<sub>0.005</sub> is 0.005, the area to the left of *z*<sub>0.005</sub> is 0.9950. We search for the area 0.9950 in [Figure 12.2 \"Cumulative Normal Probability\"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f02). The closest entries in the table are 0.9949 and 0.9951, corresponding to *z*-values 2.57 and 2.58. Since 0.995 is halfway between 0.9949 and 0.9951 we use the average 2.575 of the *z*-values for *z*<sub>0.005</sub>.

### Example 2

Use [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) to find the number $z_{\alpha / 2}$ needed in construction of a confidence interval:

1.  when the level of confidence is 90%;
2.  when the level of confidence is 99%.

Solution:

1.  In the next section we will learn about a continuous random variable that has a probability distribution called the Student *t*-distribution. [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) gives the value *t<sub>c</sub>* that cuts off a right tail of area *c* for different values of *c*. The last line of that table, the one whose heading is the symbol $\infty$ for infinity and $\lbrack z\rbrack$, gives the corresponding *z*-value *z<sub>c</sub>* that cuts off a right tail of the same area *c*. In particular, *z*<sub>0.05</sub> is the number in that row and in the column with the heading *t*<sub>0.05</sub>. We read off directly that $z_{0.05} = 1.645.$
2.  In [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) *z*<sub>0.005</sub> is the number in the last row and in the column headed *t*<sub>0.005</sub>, namely 2.576.

[Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) can be used to find *z<sub>c</sub>* only for those values of *c* for which there is a column with the heading *t<sub>c</sub>* appearing in the table; otherwise we must use [Figure 12.2 \"Cumulative Normal Probability\"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f02) in reverse. But when it can be done it is both faster and more accurate to use the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) to find *z<sub>c</sub>* than it is to do so using [Figure 12.2 \"Cumulative Normal Probability\"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f02) in reverse.

### Example 3

A sample of size 49 has sample mean 35 and sample standard deviation 14. Construct a 98% confidence interval for the population mean using this information. Interpret its meaning.

Solution:

For confidence level 98%, $\alpha = 1 - 0.98 = 0.02$, so $z_{\alpha / 2} = z_{0.01}.$ From [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we read directly that $z_{0.01} = 2.326.$ Thus

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 35 \pm 2.326\left( \frac{14}{\sqrt{49}} \right) = 35 \pm 4.652 \approx 35 \pm 4.7$$

We are 98% confident that the population mean *μ* lies in the interval $\left\lbrack {30.3,39.7} \right\rbrack$, in the sense that in repeated sampling 98% of all intervals constructed from the sample data in this manner will contain *μ*.

### Example 4

A random sample of 120 students from a large university yields mean GPA 2.71 with sample standard deviation 0.51. Construct a 90% confidence interval for the mean GPA of all students at the university.

Solution:

For confidence level 90%, $\alpha = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$, so $z_{\alpha / 2} = z_{0.05}.$ From [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we read directly that $z_{0.05} = 1.645.$ Since *n* = 120, $\overset{-}{x} = 2.71$, and *s* = 0.51,

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 2.71 \pm 1.645\left( \frac{0.51}{\sqrt{120}} \right) = 2.71 \pm 0.0766$$

One may be 90% confident that the true average GPA of all students at the university is contained in the interval $\left( {2.71 - 0.08,2.71 + 0.08} \right) = \left( {2.63,2.79} \right).$

````````````{important} Key Takeaways

- A confidence interval for a population mean is an estimate of the population mean together with an indication of reliability.
- There are different formulas for a confidence interval based on the sample size and whether or not the population standard deviation is known.
- The confidence intervals are constructed entirely from the sample data (or sample data and the population standard deviation, when it is known).
````````````

## 7.2 Small Sample Estimation of a Population Mean

````````````{note} Learning Objectives

1.  To become familiar with Student's *t*-distribution.
2.  To understand how to apply additional formulas for a confidence interval for a population mean.

````````````

The confidence interval formulas in the previous section are based on the Central Limit Theorem, the statement that for large samples $\overset{-}{X}$ is normally distributed with mean *μ* and standard deviation $\left. \mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}. \right.$ When the population mean *μ* is estimated with a small sample (*n* \< 30), the Central Limit Theorem does not apply. In order to proceed we assume that the numerical population from which the sample is taken has a normal distribution to begin with. If this condition is satisfied then when the population standard deviation *σ* is known the old formula $\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \mathit{\sigma}/\sqrt{n} \right)$ can still be used to construct a $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)$% confidence interval for *μ*.

If the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample size *n* is small then when we substitute the sample standard deviation *s* for *σ* the normal approximation is no longer valid. The solution is to use a different distribution, called Student's *t*-distribution[^stats-4] **with** $n\text{-}1$ degrees of freedom[^stats-5]. Student's *t*-distribution is very much like the standard normal distribution in that it is centered at 0 and has the same qualitative bell shape, but it has heavier tails than the standard normal distribution does, as indicated by [Figure 7.5 \"Student's t-Distribution\"](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s02-f05), in which the curve (in brown) that meets the dashed vertical line at the lowest point is the *t*-distribution with two degrees of freedom, the next curve (in blue) is the *t*-distribution with five degrees of freedom, and the thin curve (in red) is the standard normal distribution. As also indicated by the figure, as the sample size *n* increases, Student's *t*-distribution ever more closely resembles the standard normal distribution. Although there is a different *t*-distribution for every value of *n*, once the sample size is 30 or more it is typically acceptable to use the standard normal distribution instead, as we will always do in this text.

``````{figure} /images/redrawn/fig-07-05.svg
:alt: Three bell-shaped curves centered at 0: the standard normal (red), the t-distribution with 5 degrees of freedom (blue), and with 2 degrees of freedom (brown). The fewer the degrees of freedom, the lower the peak and the heavier the tails.
:name: src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s02-f05

Figure 7.5 Student's *t*-Distribution
``````

Just as the symbol *z<sub>c</sub>* stands for the value that cuts off a right tail of area *c* in the standard normal distribution, so the symbol *t<sub>c</sub>* stands for the value that cuts off a right tail of area *c* in Student's *t*-distribution. This gives us the following confidence interval formulas.

````````````{important} Small Sample $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)\%$ Confidence Interval for a Population Mean

If *σ* is known: $\overset{-}{x} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{\mathit{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$

If *σ* is unknown: $\overset{-}{x} \pm t_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$ (degrees of freedom $df = n\text{-}1$)

The population must be normally distributed.

A sample is considered small when *n* \< 30.

````````````

To use the new formula we use the line in [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) that corresponds to the relevant sample size.

### Example 5

A sample of size 15 drawn from a normally distributed population has sample mean 35 and sample standard deviation 14. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population mean, and interpret its meaning.

Solution:

Since the population is normally distributed, the sample is small, and the population standard deviation is unknown, the formula that applies is

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm t_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$$

Confidence level 95% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.025. \right.$ Since the sample size is *n* = 15, there are $n\text{-}1 = 14$ degrees of freedom. By [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) $t_{0.025} = 2.145.$ Thus

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm t_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 35 \pm 2.145\left( \frac{14}{\sqrt{15}} \right) = 35 \pm 7.8$$

One may be 95% confident that the true value of *μ* is contained in the interval $\left( {35 - 7.8,35 + 7.8} \right) = \left( {27.2,42.8} \right).$

### Example 6

A random sample of 12 students from a large university yields mean GPA 2.71 with sample standard deviation 0.51. Construct a 90% confidence interval for the mean GPA of all students at the university. Assume that the numerical population of GPAs from which the sample is taken has a normal distribution.

Solution:

Since the population is normally distributed, the sample is small, and the population standard deviation is unknown, the formula that applies is

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm t_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$$

Confidence level 90% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.05. \right.$ Since the sample size is *n* = 12, there are $n\text{-}1 = 11$ degrees of freedom. By [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) $t_{0.05} = 1.796.$ Thus

$$\overset{-}{x} \pm t_{\alpha / 2}\left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 2.71 \pm 1.796\left( \frac{0.51}{\sqrt{12}} \right) = 2.71 \pm 0.26$$

One may be 90% confident that the true average GPA of all students at the university is contained in the interval $\left( {2.71 - 0.26,2.71 + 0.26} \right) = \left( {2.45,2.97} \right).$

Compare [Note 7.9 \"Example 4\"](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s01-n06) in [Section 7.1 \"Large Sample Estimation of a Population Mean\"](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s01) and [Note 7.16 \"Example 6\"](/chapters/ch-07-estimation.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch07-s02-n04). ## 7.3 Large Sample Estimation of a Population Proportion

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To understand how to apply the formula for a confidence interval for a population proportion.

````````````

Since from [Section 6.3 \"The Sample Proportion\"](/chapters/ch-06-sampling-distributions.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch06-s03) in [Chapter 6 \"Sampling Distributions\"](/chapters/ch-06-sampling-distributions.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch06) we know the mean, standard deviation, and sampling distribution of the sample proportion $\hat{p}$, the ideas of the previous two sections can be applied to produce a confidence interval for a population proportion. Here is the formula.

````````````{important} Large Sample $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)\%$ Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

$$\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{n}}$$

A sample is large if the interval $\left\lbrack {p\text{-}3\,\mathit{\sigma}_{\hat{P}},\, p + 3\,\mathit{\sigma}_{\hat{P}}} \right\rbrack$ lies wholly within the interval $\lbrack 0,1\rbrack.$

````````````

In actual practice the value of *p* is not known, hence neither is $\mathit{\sigma}_{\hat{P}}.$ In that case we substitute the known quantity $\hat{p}$ for *p* in making the check; this means checking that the interval

$$\left\lbrack {\hat{p}\text{-}3\,\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{n}},\,\hat{p} + 3\,\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{n}}} \right\rbrack$$

lies wholly within the interval $\lbrack 0,1\rbrack.$

### Example 7

To estimate the proportion of students at a large college who are female, a random sample of 120 students is selected. There are 69 female students in the sample. Construct a 90% confidence interval for the proportion of all students at the college who are female.

Solution:

The proportion of students in the sample who are female is $\left. \hat{p} = 69/ 120 = 0.575. \right.$

Confidence level 90% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.05. \right.$ From the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we obtain $z_{0.05} = 1.645.$

Thus

$$\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha / 2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{n}} = 0.575 \pm 1.645\sqrt{\frac{(0.575)(0.425)}{120}} = 0.575 \pm 0.074$$

One may be 90% confident that the true proportion of all students at the college who are female is contained in the interval $\left( {0.575 - 0.074,0.575 + 0.074} \right) = \left( {0.501,0.649} \right).$

````````````{important} Key Takeaways

- We have a single formula for a confidence interval for a population proportion, which is valid when the sample is large.
- The condition that a sample be large is not that its size *n* be at least 30, but that the density function fits inside the interval $\lbrack 0,1\rbrack.$
````````````

## 7.4 Sample Size Considerations

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To learn how to apply formulas for estimating the size sample that will be needed in order to construct a confidence interval for a population mean or proportion that meets given criteria.

````````````

Sampling is typically done with a set of clear objectives in mind. For example, an economist might wish to estimate the mean yearly income of workers in a particular industry at 90% confidence and to within \$500. Since sampling costs time, effort, and money, it would be useful to be able to estimate the smallest size sample that is likely to meet these criteria.

### Estimating *μ*

The confidence interval formulas for estimating a population mean *μ* have the form $\overset{-}{x} \pm E.$ When the population standard deviation *σ* is known,

$$E = \frac{z_{\alpha / 2}\mathit{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The number $z_{\alpha / 2}$ is determined by the desired level of confidence. To say that we wish to estimate the mean to within a certain number of units means that we want the margin of error *E* to be no larger than that number. Thus we obtain the minimum sample size needed by solving the displayed equation for *n*.

````````````{important} Minimum Sample Size for Estimating a Population Mean

The estimated minimum sample size *n* needed to estimate a population mean *μ* to within *E* units at $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)$% confidence is

$$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\mathit{\sigma}^{2}}{E^{2}}\quad(\text{rounded}\, up)$$

````````````

To apply the formula we must have prior knowledge of the population in order to have an estimate of its standard deviation *σ*. In all the examples and exercises the population standard deviation will be given.

### Example 8

Find the minimum sample size necessary to construct a 99% confidence interval for *μ* with a margin of error *E* = 0.2. Assume that the population standard deviation is *σ* = 1.3.

Solution:

Confidence level 99% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.99 = 0.01$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.005. \right.$ From the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we obtain $z_{0.005} = 2.576.$ Thus

$$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\mathit{\sigma}^{2}}{E^{2}} = \frac{(2.576)^{2}(1.3)^{2}}{(0.2)^{2}} = 280.361536$$

which we round up to 281, since it is impossible to take a fractional observation.

### Example 9

An economist wishes to estimate, with a 95% confidence interval, the yearly income of welders with at least five years experience to within \$1,000. He estimates that the range of incomes is no more than \$24,000, so using the Empirical Rule he estimates the population standard deviation to be about one-sixth as much, or about \$4,000. Find the estimated minimum sample size required.

Solution:

Confidence level 95% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.025. \right.$ From the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we obtain $z_{0.025} = 1.960.$

To say that the estimate is to be "to within \$1,000" means that *E* = 1000. Thus

$$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\mathit{\sigma}^{2}}{E^{2}} = \frac{(1.960)^{2}(4000)^{2}}{(1000)^{2}} = 61.4656$$

which we round up to 62.

### Estimating *p*

The confidence interval formula for estimating a population proportion *p* is $\hat{p} \pm E$, where

$$E = z_{\alpha / 2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{n}}$$

The number $z_{\alpha / 2}$ is determined by the desired level of confidence. To say that we wish to estimate the population proportion to within a certain number of percentage points means that we want the margin of error *E* to be no larger than that number (expressed as a proportion). Thus we obtain the minimum sample size needed by solving the displayed equation for *n*.

````````````{important} Minimum Sample Size for Estimating a Population Proportion

The estimated minimum sample size *n* needed to estimate a population proportion *p* to within *E* at $100\left( {1 - \alpha} \right)$% confidence is

$$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{E^{2}}\quad(\text{rounded}\, up)$$

````````````

There is a dilemma here: the formula for estimating how large a sample to take contains the number $\hat{p}$, which we know only after we have taken the sample. There are two ways out of this dilemma. Typically the researcher will have some idea as to the value of the population proportion *p*, hence of what the sample proportion $\hat{p}$ is likely to be. For example, if last month 37% of all voters thought that state taxes are too high, then it is likely that the proportion with that opinion this month will not be dramatically different, and we would use the value 0.37 for $\hat{p}$ in the formula.

The second approach to resolving the dilemma is simply to replace $\hat{p}$ in the formula by 0.5. This is because if $\hat{p}$ is large then $1 - \hat{p}$ is small, and vice versa, which limits their product to a maximum value of 0.25, which occurs when $\hat{p} = 0.5.$ This is called the most conservative estimate[^stats-6], since it gives the largest possible estimate of *n*.

### Example 10

Find the necessary minimum sample size to construct a 98% confidence interval for *p* with a margin of error *E* = 0.05,

1.  assuming that no prior knowledge about *p* is available; and
2.  assuming that prior studies suggest that *p* is about 0.1.

Solution:

Confidence level 98% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.98 = 0.02$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.01. \right.$ From the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we obtain $z_{0.01} = 2.326.$

1.  Since there is no prior knowledge of *p* we make the most conservative estimate that $\hat{p} = 0.5.$ Then

    $$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{E^{2}} = \frac{(2.326)^{2}(0.5)\left( {1 - 0.5} \right)}{0.05^{2}} = 541.0276$$

    which we round up to 542.

2.  Since *p* ≈ 0.1 we estimate $\hat{p}$ by 0.1, and obtain

    $$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{E^{2}} = \frac{(2.326)^{2}(0.1)\left( {1 - 0.1} \right)}{0.05^{2}} = 194.769936$$

    which we round up to 195.

### Example 11

A dermatologist wishes to estimate the proportion of young adults who apply sunscreen regularly before going out in the sun in the summer. Find the minimum sample size required to estimate the proportion to within three percentage points, at 90% confidence.

Solution:

Confidence level 90% means that $\alpha = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$ so $\left. \alpha/ 2 = 0.05. \right.$ From the last line of [Figure 12.3 \"Critical Values of \"](/appendices/appendix.md#src-fwk-shafer-ch12-f03) we obtain $z_{0.05} = 1.645.$

Since there is no prior knowledge of *p* we make the most conservative estimate that $\hat{p} = 0.5.$ To estimate "to within three percentage points" means that *E* = 0.03. Then

$$n = \frac{\left( z_{\alpha / 2} \right)^{2}\hat{p}\left( {1 - \hat{p}} \right)}{E^{2}} = \frac{(1.645)^{2}(0.5)\left( {1 - 0.5} \right)}{0.03^{2}} = 751.6736111$$

which we round up to 752.

````````````{important} Key Takeaways

- If the population standard deviation *σ* is known or can be estimated, then the minimum sample size needed to obtain a confidence interval for the population mean with a given maximum error of the estimate and a given level of confidence can be estimated.
- The minimum sample size needed to obtain a confidence interval for a population proportion with a given maximum error of the estimate and a given level of confidence can always be estimated. If there is prior knowledge of the population proportion *p* then the estimate can be sharpened.
````````````
